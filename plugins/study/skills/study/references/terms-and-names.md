# Instrument — terms and names

**The inventory of a unit: what named things it carries, what each one is, and which of them bear weight.**

Distinct from the toolkit. The toolkit analyses an argument; this gets the user hold of a unit's vocabulary and cast quickly, and it is what makes the map worth clicking — a term is a leaf, its family a branch, its gloss the detail that opens.

**Runs at any unit, in either mode.** Offer it when a unit is technical, prosopographically dense, or in a field where the user is junior. It is not automatic and not always wanted.

---

## ⭐ The three jobs, and the first two are opposite

**Ask which situation you are in before running it.** The same instrument does unlike things.

| | **Unfamiliar field** *(one where the user is junior)* | **The user's own fields** *(where they are senior)* |
|---|---|---|
| The situation | The field has an established vocabulary the user does not yet hold | The established vocabulary is already theirs |
| What helps | Something close to a **glossary**, delivered **before the reading** as preparation for it | **Sense-tracking**: where *this author* bends a term the user already owns, and where the author shifts senses without saying so |
| The question each entry answers | *What is this, and what work does it do here?* | *How is he using it, and how does that differ from standard use?* |
| Failure mode | Terse listing that assumes the user can supply the sense | Defining what they have taught for years |

⚠ **Do not run the glossary job in the user's own fields.** A definition of *ratio* delivered to a specialist is noise; the value there is entirely in the divergence between the ordinary sense and this author's.

> ### ⭐ The third case: the user's field, someone else's genre — and it is the highest-yield of the three
>
> **A source can sit squarely in the user's field and be written by someone who does not work in it** — a journalist, a memoirist, an ethnographer, an outsider polemicist. Then their vocabulary is in play but *not on their terms*, and neither job above fits: a glossary is noise, and sense-tracking a fellow specialist's careful divergence is the wrong expectation, because this author is not diverging carefully. **Such authors are using the user's words for their own purposes, and often without noticing.**
>
> **What the entry answers here:** *what is this author doing with a word he did not inherit the way I did?* Three things worth catching:
>
> - **An unmarked shift** — the same term carrying two incompatible senses in one book, with nothing acknowledging the change. **This is the find.** *(Levin: "grace" as the Salvation Army's unconditional love without judgement in one chapter, and as "the whitewash process by which no one has to be accountable" in another. The book's whole moral architecture sits in that gap, and she never names it.)*
> - **A discipline's word borrowed by another discipline** — *"fundamentalist"* used sociologically, as a genus with a movement inside it, where the user would hear a specific historical controversy. Say which sense is running.
> - **Terms collapsed that the user keeps apart** — *(Levin uses "Pentecostal" and "charismatic" interchangeably and never mentions the charismatic renewal in the historic churches.)* That is a fact about the source's reach, not only about its vocabulary.
>
> ⚠ **Do not correct the author into the user's usage.** The point is not that she is wrong; it is that the word is doing different work here, and the user needs to know which work before they lift anything. **Report the usage, name the gap, leave the verdict to them.**

## Which items

Four signs that a term matters to the author. The third is the highest-yield and the easiest to detect.

1. **Authorial stress** — italics, quotation marks, an explicit definition, or a discussion of the word's senses.
2. **Presence in the foundational claims** — a term load-bearing in what the rest rests on, whether or not it is ever defined.
3. ⭐ **The author quarrels with others about it.** Where he reports how others use a word and says why he uses it otherwise, that word makes a great difference to him. *(Whitby preferring "knowledge-based system" to "expert system" is exactly this.)*
4. **Structural inheritance** — from the title, the chapter headings, and the unit's own whole.

**In an unfamiliar field, add a fifth:** what the *field* uses, not only what this author stresses. The user needs the landscape, not just this book's corner of it.

**Selection criterion, as everywhere: load-bearing, not interesting.** A surprising fact is a decoy; it takes the attention that belongs to the sentence carrying the argument.

## How much explanation

> ### ⭐ Nothing appears as a bare name.
>
> A term with a page anchor and no clause of sense is a **pointer, not information** — it forces the user to ask for what should have been there, and a list of them is unreadable however accurate.

Three tiers, and the first is a floor rather than a target:

- **Every item** carries at least **one clause** saying what it is. *"**Abduction** — inference to the most plausible explanation of the symptoms."*
- **★ load-bearing items** get two or three sentences: what it is, what work it does here, and the sentence or figure the author uses to teach it.
- **Elaboration on request.** The user will ask for the ones they want opened, and that request is itself useful information about where they are. Answer it at length, in plain prose, for someone without the technical background.

**Register.** This is the place the executive voice creeps back in — a dense list of names with anchors and no prose reads as machine output talking to itself. Write it as you would say it to a colleague who has not read the book. Full sentences for anything that carries weight.

## Types

The commonest are terms and names. The instrument is not limited to them, and the user may ask for others.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Terms** | the working vocabulary; the technical apparatus |
| **People** | who they are, and what they gave the argument — not biography. *(For a large cast, the people-map is the better artifact: grouped by role, with a relationship axis)* |
| **Systems, programs, works** | what it was, when, and what it demonstrated |
| **Dates and years** | for historical sources: a chronology, where sequence is itself the argument |
| **Cited sources** | what the author is reading, and which of them he argues with |
| **Places, institutions, schools** | where a position is anchored |

Take any other request in the same shape.

## Grouping — this is what the map renders

**Group by family, not alphabetically, and name the families.** In the Whitby run, ch. 2 split into *search* and *knowledge*, ch. 3 into *neural nets* and *evolutionary computing* — and those groupings were the most useful structural thing the chapter descents produced, because they are the author's real conceptual divisions rather than his exposition order.

Each family gets a line saying what holds it together.

> ### ⭐ Give each family a core, and hang the terms off it by what they do
>
> A family is not a list. Name the **core idea**, then group its terms by their function relative to it — *its machinery · the limit it runs into · the spin-offs the limit produced*, or whatever the material actually shows.
>
> **Why it matters beyond tidiness:** a flat list renders as a grid, and a grid is decoration, since any item could sit anywhere without loss (`maps.md`, "position must mean something"). Give the family a core and the arrangement starts carrying information.
>
> **And it can turn up a finding.** On Whitby all four families proved to share one shape — *core · machinery · limit (· spin-offs)* — which is the book's own refrain appearing four times at family scale. **When an arrangement makes an argument visible, say so, and say whose it is.**

**Where the author warns that two families rename each other, that is a finding, not a tidying problem.** Whitby says biologically inspired AI "has shamelessly plundered the biological sciences for jargon" (p. 42) and cashes it out sixty pages later; the inventory should carry both ends.

## What lands where

**In the node**, under the unit it belongs to: the families, each item with its anchor and its clause, ★ on the load-bearing ones, and elaborations where they were asked for. This is the structure the map projects — families become branches and items become leaves, so an inventory written well needs no reformatting to render.

**Layer 1 throughout.** A gloss is a reading of how the author uses a word, not a quotation of him; where the difference matters, put his words beside it.

**A gap in the source belongs here too.** Where the standard rationale for a concept is simply absent from the book — as Herbert Simon and *satisficing* are absent from Whitby's heuristics — say so at the entry. That is a fact about the source and it is worth more than another definition.
