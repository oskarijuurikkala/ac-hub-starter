# The toolkit — analytic instruments

**The analytic instrument set** — the ways of seeing picked up when the question is *what is actually happening here, and how does the argument work?*

**Available at L2 and L3, single source or batch.** Not gated to L3. At L2 the instruments show *more in the parts than their topics* — that this part is the load-bearing inference the rest depends on, that those two are convergent support for one claim, that this one is where the author answers the anticipated objection. That is the parts seen through an argumentation lens rather than as a table of contents. The same instruments run at whole-book, whole-article and batch scale. *(Elements of this may serve other contexts too; left as a future design question, not wired here.)*

**It is called a toolkit, not a set of lenses, deliberately.** A lens is something the AI puts on and looks through, invisibly; a toolkit is picked up by the two of you together. Lenses that fire automatically, gated by a routing table, report residue, so a completed analysis never has to exist. The discipline below prevents that.

---

## ⭐⭐ The discipline — how the toolkit is used

> **Read the material first. Then ask which of these ways of seeing would show you something you do not already see in it. Propose those — say what each would surface *here*, and why — and wait. The instruments are ways of seeing, not procedures to run. Where none fits, say so briefly and move on.**

This is the whole thing. Everything below serves it. Three rules make it hold:

1. **Propose, then wait.** Never put on an instrument invisibly and report what it turned up. Name the two or three that fit *this* material, say what each would show and why, and let the user choose. Same discipline as batch mode's choice-of-axis: the highest-imputation-risk act is choosing the frame, so it is checked, never adopted on the AI's own conviction.

2. **Connect at read-time, from the material — never route by genre.** The motive-map below says where an instrument *tends* to earn its cost, with a *because*. That is an orientation to look, not an assignment. The connection is made by reading *this* text and recognising that *here* the whole chapter leans on one unbacked warrant — not by looking up "patristic text → these instruments." A proposal that reads *"this is disputational, so —"* has routed; a proposal that reads *"the argument turns entirely on one step the author never defends, so warrant-excavation would show where —"* has connected. The second is the job.

3. **Every instrument carries a completion condition** — what it produces, and how you know it is finished. *An operation with no test for being finished gets skipped, and only its visible residue gets produced.* Each card states its own. Where you reach for something not in the repertoire, name it, say what it would produce and how you would know it was done, and let the user decide whether to spend the turn — do not run an unnamed operation and report its residue.

> ### ⚠ Propose what fits; do not build the case against what doesn't
> The repair cuts *both* ways. Firing an instrument invisibly is one failure; **cataloguing at length which instruments you are declining, and why, is the same failure wearing the opposite coat** — a routing table run in negative, and it pre-decides from genre exactly what rule 2 forbids. So: propose the two or three that fit. If a natural expectation does not fit *this* text, one clause is enough (*"no opponent here, so nothing dialectical"*) — never a paragraph of instruments-considered-and-rejected. **What earns space is the fit you found, not the fits you ruled out.**

**Who performs an instrument** is the division-of-labour test (SKILL.md): does performing it generate information its output does not contain? Constructing an argument does — where the user cannot build it locates their gap — so in a formative session they build it first, you respond. Tracking a term's senses does not — you do it. *When the user has delegated ("run a draft of all three") or already owns the text, you perform it and hand it over; note the labour point once if it bears, not on every card.*

**The selection criterion, across all of them: load-bearing, not interesting.** Pause over what *puzzles*, not what is *striking*. In an old source the surprising facts are the decoy; they attract the attention that belongs to the sentences carrying the argument.

## The repertoire

Each card: **what it lets you see · its motive (where it tends to earn its cost, and why) · the move, carrying its own completion condition.** The cards are ways of seeing, not scripts; read the motive, look at the material, propose if it fits. Where an instrument has an edge it must not overrun, that limit lives *inside the move* — an instrument knowing where to stop and hand off, not a filter for pre-declining it.

The two marked ⭐ are specified and proven in use; the rest are candidates whose implemented-well form the trials are still teaching. That is a fact about how much we have tested, not a ranking — none runs by default; all are proposed the same way.

---

### ⭐ Argument construction

**Sees:** the argument a text is actually making, assembled across paragraphs where the author left it implicit. Where an author already numbers his own premises, the yield is one level up — construct *his* argument, or the argument of the whole chapter, which the numbered pieces do not themselves state.

**Motive:** expository material — where the point is to establish a claim by reasoning, and the reasoning is what the user needs. On a set of parallel arguments (several *viae*, several proofs), the high-yield move is **comparative**: build them all to one template and see where the shared shape holds and where it breaks — a divergence the running prose hides.

**The move:** locate the key sentences; get the propositions they contain; construct the argument, assembling across paragraphs the author did not make visible; mark supplied steps as supplied (`[IMPLICIT]`, in their most charitable form). **Completion — the nutshell test:** *if it cannot be put in a nutshell, the argument has not been constructed* — a sprawl means the structure has not yet been found. On a comparative run, each member nutshelled, and the shared-or-divergent shape named.

---

### ⭐ Controlling principles

**Sees:** what an author is committed to and may never state anywhere — Plato on conversation, Aristotle on happiness as the *whole* of the good, Kant on the limits of mind.

**Motive:** any source deep enough to have an unstated commitment governing it; especially the ones the user returns to across a corpus. ⚠ **Name the principle from *this text's own operations*, not from a grammar you bring.** Reaching for a familiar frame — even the user's own deep grammar — and laying it over a source where the author conceives the matter differently is the imputation the whole skill guards against: a structure of yours made to look like a structure of his. State what *this* author is committed to, in terms the text earns.

**The move:** name a candidate principle as a claim that could be wrong; gather the passages that bear it and — the honest half — the passages that *strain* it. **Completion (per session):** a candidate principle stated contestably, with its bearing passages and its straining passages. Its findings accrete across sessions rather than completing within one, so the condition is per-session, not final.

---

### Logic chains — how support flows

**Sees:** how support actually flows to a claim, which is *not* the same as how the parts divide. Parts are components; a chain is a dependency. Serial (A→B→C, failure anywhere breaks everything downstream) · linked (premises work only jointly) · convergent (independent reasons each separately support — a cumulative case; **convergent structure is not a weakness**, though critics often treat it so — Newman's convergence of probabilities is the paradigm) · divergent (one premise, several claims — watch for equivocation across branches).

**Motive:** where what the user needs is *where the argument depends on what* — the load-bearing joint, the step that if removed collapses the most. Can serve L2 as a special case: it clarifies *why* the parts sit as they do when the division is argumentative rather than topical. A distinct instrument from L2's parts-and-whole, not a synonym. The sharpest yield is often across a set: several arguments that *look* independent may share one joint, so that removing it collapses them together — the convergent picture overstating how many independent supports there really are.

**The move:** for the unit, identify the claim; classify how its supports relate; mark the load-bearing joint; explicate enthymemes in their most charitable form (`[IMPLICIT]`). Where the text's organisation is not inferential at all, sketch the *movement* of the exposition instead and say plainly it is not an inference structure. **Completion:** the support type of every load-bearing step is classified, and the joint whose removal collapses the most is named.

### The shape of a dispute — the moves made, and the distinctions that turn them

*(This is the reframed quaestio instrument. The medieval structure — objection, sed contra, respondeo, reply — is one instance of it, not its definition; the instrument is generic, from patristic to modern.)*

**Sees:** the shape of a disagreement — not merely who holds what, but *what kind of move each side makes*, and where the two cases actually meet. Often the affirmative and the negative run on **different premises** and touch at only one point; naming that is the finding. And it sees the **distinction as an argumentative move** — where a text advances, or dissolves a disagreement, by disambiguating a term into two senses (the scholastic *distinguo*, but the move is everywhere): which sense is affirmed, which denied, and whether the distinction *saves* a claim or *breaks* it. A distinction analysis is valuable across the board — a patristic text equalising two things by distinguishing senses of a word is doing it as much as a *quaestio* is.

**Motive:** any text that contests a position or answers objections — and, more broadly, any text whose progress turns on distinguishing senses of a key term. Every determination implies the doubt it answers; recovering that doubt, and the moves on each side, shows what the text actually establishes — often much less, or something other, than the reader assumes.

**The move:** reconstruct the dispute — what is genuinely at issue (state it as the text's own question, not a narrower one you supply); the moves the negative makes and the moves the affirmative makes; and *where, if anywhere, they engage the same ground* rather than passing each other on different premises. Where a distinction is doing work, name the term, its two senses, which is affirmed and which denied, and whether it saves or breaks the claim. **Completion:** the question at issue is stated in the text's own terms; the kinds of move on each side are named; each load-bearing distinction is named with its two senses and its effect.

> ⚠ **Do not force a resolution-shape, and do not read "the sides run on different premises" as "there is no dispute."** A question can be fully disputed at the level of its actual question while the determination proceeds by fresh premises rather than by refuting the objections on their own ground — that is normal, often typical. The finding is the *shape* of the moves, not a verdict that the dispute is absent.

### Warrant excavation — the implicit inference-licence

**Sees:** the implicit inference-licence (warrant) beneath a claim–grounds pair, and what authorises it (backing). Its one irreplaceable insight is **field-dependence of backing**: the *form* of argument is invariant across fields, but the standards backing a warrant are field-specific.

**Motive:** a striking inferential leap; or two disciplines disagreeing and it is unclear where — *because* when theology and neuroscience (or theology and analytic philosophy of mind) clash, they usually disagree about what counts as adequate backing, not about the claim, and locating the disagreement there transforms it. Also potent where a text's whole verdict rests on a criterion of success the author *imports* and never defends: excavate that criterion as the load-bearing warrant.

**The move:** state claim and grounds in the author's terms; formulate the warrant as a general licence (*data like D entitle one to conclude claims like C because ___*); ask what field-specific backing authorises it and whether the author states it (a warrant present in his system but **absent from this text** is a `[GAP]` in the precise sense — the place hasty readers stumble); check whether the qualifier matches the strength of the grounds. **Completion:** the warrant is made explicit and its backing named or marked absent; for a cross-disciplinary clash, the disagreement is located at claim or at backing. ⚠ **This instrument locates the seam; it does not weigh.** It is elicitation, not an evaluation calculus and not a storage format — when the backing turns out to be a participation-metaphysics or a way-of-seeing, its job is to name that seam and **hand back** to the instrument that reads it (constructive-participatory, controlling principles), not to adjudicate it.

### Scheme + critical questions

**Sees:** the *kind* of defeasible inference in play, and the stock questions that test it. Source-based (expert opinion; position to know; authority of tradition) · analogy · example · sign · consequences · best explanation · cause-to-effect vs correlation-to-cause · verbal classification · precedent · argument from ignorance. Each scheme carries a fixed set of critical questions that function as its defeasibility conditions.

**Motive:** prose that leans on a recognisable pattern of inference — the critical questions are a ready-made interrogation protocol, content-sensitive where warrant-excavation is purely structural.

*Kept for exploration.* What this looks like implemented well — how the scheme is named without the AI silently gating it, how many critical questions are worth putting — is genuinely uncertain and is what the trials are for. Do not over-specify it; propose it, try it, and put what it did into the study-log as a `#learn` line.

**The move (provisional):** name the scheme in play (if two are candidates, name both and interrogate with both sets rather than guessing); put its critical questions to the material; each critical question the source leaves unanswered is a finding. **Completion (provisional):** the operative scheme is named, and its critical questions are answered from the text or marked unanswered.

### Attack-typing — where an objection lands

**Sees:** *where* an objection lands. Three types: undermining (attacks a premise) · undercutting (attacks the inference — grants the premises, denies they support the conclusion here) · rebutting (attacks the conclusion directly with a counter-argument).

**Motive:** whenever the source reports a dispute, answers objections, or the user is weighing its position. The single highest-yield question in the literature relative to its cost: *is this attacking the premise, the inference, or the conclusion?* — because the common failure is a rebutting-style response to an undercutting objection (restating the conclusion louder instead of defending the inference), and the type-mismatch is invisible until named. It also catches a whole *literature* answering the wrong type — defenders restating that a thesis holds when the live objection was to the inference, not the thesis.

**The move:** for each objection–response pair, type the objection; check whether the response answers *that type*; note objections the author types wrongly. **Completion:** every objection–response pair in the unit has its objection typed and its response checked for type-match. *(Informal use only; the formal calculus buys nothing here.)*

### Pragma-dialectical — what the author is *doing*

**Sees:** what the author is *doing*, not just asserting — the text read as one side of an implicit critical discussion with an anticipated rational critic. Four stages: confrontation (what difference of opinion?) · opening (what shared starting points are assumed?) · argumentation · concluding. **Strategic manoeuvring:** the author's simultaneous pursuit of being reasonable and being effective — watch where effectiveness wins (burden-shifting, straw-manning the antagonist, declaring victory prematurely).

**Motive:** polemics, controversial documents, texts with a visible opponent named or anticipated — *because* it also detects the *unnamed* interlocutor a text is really answering, which the surface does not declare.

**The move:** reconstruct the difference of opinion (who denies what?); list the starting points the author treats as shared and flag those the real opponent would not concede; identify the strongest anticipated objection and how it is handled; note the strategic manoeuvring. **Completion:** the difference of opinion is reconstructed, the assumed-shared starting points are listed with the contestable ones flagged, and the handling of the strongest anticipated objection is named. *(Reconstruction is interpretively contestable — keep conclusions about the author's intent modest, marked `[INT]`.)*

---

### Qualitative Bayesian — how much this evidence should move belief

**Sees:** graded evidential strength — how much a body of evidence *should* move belief, where logic's valid/invalid binary cannot.

**Motive:** empirical papers; cumulative-case arguments; science–theology disputes about evidential force — the live dispute is frequently about how much a body of evidence should shift a prior. ⚠ **Qualitative only — never invent numbers.** And do not import a probabilistic-cumulative-case frame onto a text that does not carry one: reading several classical proofs as independent Bayesian supports is a modern reconstruction (Swinburne), not what a medieval author thought — check that the frame is *in the text* before using it.

**The move — the three disciplining questions:** *likelihood contrast* — what would this evidence look like if the claim were false? (about the same → weak, regardless of rhetorical weight). *Prior sensitivity* — does the argument's force depend on a prior the reader may not share? (say so; many apologetic and anti-apologetic arguments differ only here). *Aggregation* — do the multiple evidences count independently, or share a source/assumption (double-counting)? Is source reliability itself in dispute? **Completion:** the three questions are put to the load-bearing evidential claim, and each is answered or marked unanswerable from the text.

### Constructive-participatory — the non-disputational register

**Sees:** the structures a non-disputational text *does* have — which are real and analysable, and which the argument-instruments will silently reformat into claim-and-warrant if this one does not read them on their own terms first. Much of the user's own corpus may be here; this is the least-standardised instrument, and the trials will develop it most.

**Detection:** exposition ordered by a text being interpreted rather than a thesis defended; doxological or contemplative register; movement by intensification, figure and resonance rather than inference; no opponent.

**The move — produce, instead of an argument map:**
- **a movement-sketch** — the order and logic of the exposition;
- **the senses of the text** — where Scripture organises the work, what functions literally, typologically, morally, anagogically (a structural question with checkable answers, not a devotional gloss);
- **the grammar of participation** — for each load-bearing figure, ask **"the figure is a figure *of what*?"** Where there is a statable non-figural truth underneath that the image merely illustrates, the figure is a teaching image, discardable once grasped. **Where there is *no* statable "what" underneath — where the figure's own working *is* the claim, and restating it flatly loses it — the figure is doing constitutive work.** *And the binary itself may collapse: in some authors (Maximus is the type-case) symbol and metaphysics are not rivals — the symbol is how the ontology is stated, so "illustrative or ontological?" has no clean answer, and* **the collapse is itself the finding** *— it tells you something specific about how this author's account of symbol works.* Do not force the pick; report the collapse where it happens. Where analogical predication is genuinely in play, name whether it is analogy of attribution or of proportionality.
- **a figural inventory** — the network of images (light, harmony, book, ladder, garment, garden) and what each consistently carries; where inherited (name the source) vs transformed;
- and where the text *does* briefly argue disputationally (anti-heretical asides), flag it for the argument-instruments — and if a single inferential joint carries real weight, warrant-excavation may *locate* it (then hand back here).

**Completion:** a movement-sketch, and for each load-bearing figure the participation-grammar worked — figure-of-what named, or the binary's collapse recorded as the finding. The figures are inventoried, not merely admired.

*(Provenance note: the "figure of what?" probe replaces an earlier "illustrative vs ontological — the characteristic modern error" framing that a Maximus trial found mis-fired — it drove to the right place by being wrong, forcing the question rather than answering it. The binary-collapse outcome is that trial's yield. Carried here 2026-09-22; expect further revision from use.)*

## The motive-map — orientation, not routing

> ⚠ **These are tendencies, not routes. Read the material, recognise what it is doing, and let that suggest where to look — then check the suggestion against the actual text.** The map exists to prompt the connection, never to make it for you. A proposal that cites the map ("this is expository, so —") has routed; a proposal that cites the material ("the argument rests on one undefended step, so —") has connected. Only the second is the discipline.

**When a unit is doing this** → **these instruments tend to earn their cost, because —**

- **establishing a claim by reasoning** (expository, argumentative) → argument construction · logic chains — *the reasoning is the content, and it is usually spread across paragraphs the author never gathered; on parallel arguments, whether they share one shape is hidden.*
- **contesting another position** (polemic, a visible or anticipated opponent) → the shape of a dispute · attack-typing · pragma-dialectical — *what each side is doing, where the objections land, and whether the parties argue the same ground or run on different premises.*
- **leaning on a hard inferential step** (a striking leap; a cross-disciplinary clash; an imported success-criterion) → warrant excavation · scheme + critical questions — *the licence is implicit and field-specific, and that is exactly where interdisciplinary disagreement hides.*
- **weighing evidence** (empirical, cumulative-case) → qualitative Bayesian · logic chains (convergent) — *the question is how much the evidence should move belief, which logic's binary cannot answer.*
- **turning on a distinction** (a term disambiguated to advance or dissolve a disagreement — scholastic or not) → the shape of a dispute — *which sense is affirmed, which denied, and whether the distinction saves or breaks the claim.*
- **building a way of seeing** (participatory, exegetical, doxological, analogical) → constructive-participatory *(reads it on its own terms before the others reformat it)* · controlling principles — *there is no thesis to reconstruct; the work is figure, participation and the senses of the text.*
- **carrying an unstated commitment across its whole length or a corpus** → controlling principles — *the governing principle is never on any single page.*

**Most real units do more than one of these**, sometimes within a paragraph — a systematic monograph with an empirical chapter, a patristic text with a polemical excursus, a single article that establishes a claim *and* answers an objection on different premises. Propose per what the unit in front of you is actually doing, not per a label for the whole.

## Recording — flags and the store

Everything the toolkit produces is **Layer 1**: not citable until the user verifies it against the source (critical edition for primary texts). It lands at the node it bears on, in the store; corrections land in the store, never in a derived view.

- `[INT]` — an interpretation read *beneath* what the source says (a reconstructed argument the author never states). Where the toolkit earns its keep, and where verification lands.
- `[IMPLICIT]` — an explicated enthymeme, in its most charitable form; verified against what the argument *needs*, not against a passage.
- `[UNCERTAIN]` — the source underdetermines; competing readings are live and you are not choosing. Name them.
- `[GAP]` — a critical-question the source never answers, a warrant left unbacked, a claim stronger than its grounds license. **The gap report is the toolkit's highest-value product for the user's own work** — it is where their contribution typically begins.
- `[ME]` — the user's own judgment about the source, verbatim, at the node it bears on.
- `[VERIFY]` a claim to check against the source · `[SUGGESTION]` an idea offered, not written in · `[CITATION NEEDED]` a reference still owed.

**A constructed structure is marked as ours.** A neutral vocabulary imposed on a debate, or a question attributed to an author he never posed, is legitimate and often necessary — and the highest-imputation-risk operation in the system, because the author's structure and ours become indistinguishable later. Mark it, without exception. *(This is the skill's standing rule, carried here unchanged.)*

**Presenting a finding:** one insight at a time, in plain language, named for what it *shows*, not for the machinery. *"The author asserts the boundary is closed but never argues it — the whole chapter leans on that assertion"* — not *"applying Toulmin element W reveals an unbacked warrant."* Name the instrument only when the user asks, or when the point is to teach the instrument itself.
