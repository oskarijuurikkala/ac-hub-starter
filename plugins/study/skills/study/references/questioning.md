# Instrument 1 — questioning

**Picked up when the mode is asked-first.** It is the withholding step made concrete: instead of handing over the interpretation, you put a small set of questions to the user first, they commit, and then they receive it.

**Why it earns its cost — four reasons, and the second is the one most easily lost:**

1. **Being asked before reading makes the reading work.** Attempting an answer, getting it wrong, then meeting the correct answer retains better than reading the correct answer first. The gap between the guess and the correction is the learning signal. **This holds even when — especially when — every answer is wrong.** Questions the user cannot yet answer are working as intended.
2. **It lets the user skim instead of read.** They do not have to work out the unity of a book, or construct its argument, unaided. They skim for three minutes, commit to a rough answer, and your response corrects and completes it. **Dialogue plus skimming substitutes for careful solitary reading**, at a fraction of the cost.
3. **A question reaches what a free statement cannot.** Asked what they expect from a book, the user can only report what they are aware of thinking. A question puts a specific choice in front of them and gets an answer to *that*.
4. **It removes the barrier to starting.** A three-minute look with a colleague waiting, where failure is pre-authorised, has nothing threatening in it.

> ### ⚠ Never diagnose the user in advance, and never narrate the instrument at them
>
> **Removed 2026-08-16, on four trials' evidence.** Earlier versions framed the instrument around catching the user *confidently wrong* — the word they would sail past, the expensive case, the dangerous cell. Two things were wrong with it.
>
> **The anticipation does not work.** Across four trials it did not pay off once; the single real finding arrived retrospectively, from the verdict table. Guesses about what will cost them, or what they are about to miss, are close to noise.
>
> **And saying it aloud is paternalising.** *"I ask because this is the case the instrument is built for — a word a reader passes over because he knows the ordinary sense"* tells the user what their own mind is about to do. It is the register fault in a new costume.
>
> **The job is to help the user understand the source, not to predict what they will misunderstand.** Ask because the question is good. Report what happened afterwards, plainly, in the verdict table. Never explain to them why a question was chosen for them.

## 1 · Interpret before questioning

**Non-negotiable, and not a formality.** Do the interpretive work of the turn (SKILL.md, "Work the question") *before writing a single question*, and **generate the questions from the interpretation rather than from the text.**

Questions generated from the text will be about particulars — sincerely intending the big picture and asking about δύναμις/ἐντελέχεια instead. Questions generated from an interpretation can be about the whole, and are still sharp, because the interpretation was built from a full reading.

**Show the user none of the interpretation yet.**

---

## 2 · The battery

**Six questions.** Seven or eight only if the source demands it — the user's answers may run long, and that is where the value is, so keep the set short rather than discouraging the elaboration. Tell them they may skip any that do not seem relevant, or answer minimally.

**They must be answerable from what the user has seen.** At L1 that means front matter, contents, introduction, conclusion, structure — even though yours are sharpened by having read everything. At a smaller unit, the headings before a chapter; the first and last paragraph before the middle. So a wrong answer is diagnostic rather than unfair.

**Include the rival readings.** Where the interpretation produced more than one, **put them to the user as a question** — two or three candidate accounts of what the source is doing, and let them pick and comment. The alternatives must be the readings that actually emerged, including the author's own stated purpose where that is a live option. Three neutral genre labels invented for the exercise is a fake choice.

⭐ **Say what the options are, without saying which is which.** *"One of these is my primary reading, one my second; the third is the author's own stated framing. I'm not saying which."* This is the invariant working inside asked-first: the user learns that the set is real and drawn from a completed reading, which is what makes committing to one worth doing, and the withholding is untouched. Trial-proven; use it.

**At least one question from the source's own shape**, outside anything the user framed. A set built only from what they told you cannot reach what they did not think to mention. *(Their framing sets what is salient, never what is true.)*

**Ask for the user's answer and a line of reasoning.** Do not ask for a confidence rating — it asks them to quantify what they often cannot, and one scale covered three unlike situations (unfamiliar field · familiar field but unread book · correct inference from thin evidence). The verdict table shows them where they were wrong, which is enough.

**Mark each `[in-text]`** (the source settles it) **or `[open]`** (genuinely contested).

⚠ **Where the answer is an *absence*** — the author never engages a question, a criterion is left unstated — **say so in the question.** Picking a presence from a contents page is a fair ask; picking an absence is not, and marking a wrong answer there as an error is unjust.

**Ask them all in one turn, then stop and wait.**

### Where the battery fires, and how it changes

| Point | Scope | What the questions are doing |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening** | the whole source, from cover / contents / preface / introduction | Getting the user into the source fast and well-aimed: what kind of book, what problem it solves, what its division scheme claims is fundamental, what they expect |
| **Before a part** | the part about to be read | Aiming *this* reading — the same move at smaller scale |
| **After a part** | the part just read | What actually changed. Where the user still cannot restate or exemplify is what needs more |
| **At depth (L3)** | a single passage | Character changes: the user often already understands it, so the value shifts toward **arguing about a reading** rather than being asked about it |

**Coverage comes from the recursion, not from long batteries.** Six questions cannot cover a book; six per part, at each scale, reach everything that matters.

## 3 · The forms kit

> ### ⭐ Two rules govern the whole kit
>
> **1 · Answerability is per question, not per battery.** Every question must be answerable from what the user has actually seen at this point. **A form's usefulness never overrides this** — that is how it fails. A term question on a chapter-2 hinge word is a good question in the wrong place if all they have read is the contents page; ask it when that chapter is the unit.
>
> **2 · Default composition: mostly multiple choice, one or two free response.** Trial-proven across four sessions. MC with a comment invited is what worked, and it works because it lets the user think creatively about a source they have barely opened. Depart from it deliberately, not by drift.

| Form | Fires at | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Multiple choice** | **any**, and the default | Fast, and it forces commitment on a specific choice the user might otherwise talk around. **The options are positions someone competent actually holds.** ⭐ **Always invite a verbal comment alongside the choice** — optional, a line or two, on why they chose it or what made the alternatives tempting. The comment is often worth more than the pick |
| **Free response** | **any**; one or two per battery | Reveals reasoning, vocabulary, and what the user does not mention. Nothing here forces a preference against it on grading cost, as it would in ordinary teaching |
| **Rival readings** | **L1** | The candidate accounts of what the unit is doing. See §2 |
| **Prediction** | **any** | *"What do you expect his answer to that objection to be?"* A wrong prediction is the point; say so if the user apologises for one |
| **On a term** | **L1 only if the term is visible in the title, contents or front matter**; otherwise L2/L3 | *"How is **he** using it, and how does that differ from standard use?"* Never *"do you know this term?"* — the first asks about the source, the second about the user |
| **On terms in an unfamiliar field** | L1–L3 | The user may not hold the vocabulary at all; basic multiple choice on key terms is a good way in, quick and low-stakes |
| **The anchored question** | **L2 / L3** | Quote one to three sentences verbatim, then ask *"why do you think the author says that?"* Asks for **rationale rather than content**. It presupposes a decision about which passage matters, so it is not an entry point at L1 |
| **The subtly false statement** | **after reading a part; L3** | Below |

### The subtly false statement

Take something correct — an interpretation of the source, or a direct quotation — **alter it so that it is inaccurate**, put it to the user, and ask what is wrong with it. They answer; you complete what they missed.

**It does something no other form does.** Multiple choice hands the user the right answer among the options. This hands them a **plausible wrong** and asks them to locate the fault, which is what reading scholarship actually involves.

**Four constraints:**

- **The alteration must be load-bearing.** A changed date is a spotting exercise; changing *"participation according to capacity"* to *"analogical predication"* tests whether the user holds the distinction. Missing it should be diagnostic, not merely inattentive.
- ⚠ **An altered quotation is marked as altered in the record, with the true text beside it.** Otherwise a doctored quote sits in a study artifact and is later read as the source's own words. This is a citation hazard nothing else in the design creates. Format in `store-template.md`.
- **Difficulty comes from subtlety**, which gives escalation a dial without a ladder.
- **State it plainly; never spring it.** *"Here is a statement about X. Something in it is wrong — what?"* The colleague version, not the trap; a gotcha grates against the understatement rule.

**Where it fires:** after reading a part — its strongest position — and at L3, where the user often already understands the passage and the value lies in arguing about accuracy.

### In fields where the user is junior

In a field where the user is junior they cannot improvise from broad knowledge as they can in their own. Options there stop being a scaffold for thinking and become an exam. **Write options that teach the landscape whether or not the user picks well** — carry enough content in each that the set orients them.

## 4 · Reading the answers

**Open with a compact verdict table** — one row per question: the user's answer, the source's, and one of *right · wrong · uncertain-and-right · open disagreement*. They should see where they stand at a glance.

**Then the discussion, errors first**, and only on the questions that repay it. Confirmatory feedback on a wrong guess is worse than none; manufactured disagreement is its own failure.

- **Right from thin evidence** — not the same as latent knowledge. The user reasoned well from little; the inference is worth checking rather than assuming.
- **Right, and past the source** — where the user's answer supplies something the book does not. Say so, and say what the book actually gives; the difference is a gap in the source.
- An **`[open]`** question answered against the author is **a disagreement, not an error** — and is candidate generative material for the thinking note.

*The table reports what happened. It does not diagnose the user, and nothing here is framed as catching them out.*

**Then give your reading of the whole, with its basis stated plainly.** *"Here is my reading, and here is how I came to it — the introduction says X, but what actually carries the book is Y in chapter 3."* Where you hold more than one, give them, and say where they conflict. Where the basis is thin, say so: that is a fact about the source.

### Escalation

Harder question after an easy answer; simpler after a strained one. **Stop at the first answer the user struggles with and name the boundary** — *"the edge here seems to be X"* — rather than climbing until they fail.

⭐ **When you name a boundary, ask whose it is.** Where the user's answers went soft, the cause may be their own gap — or **the source may stop arguing at exactly that point.**

> *"Your answers went soft exactly where the book stops arguing — which is also where it is thinnest. So it is not clear the softness was yours."*

These are opposite findings. A design that reads all uncertainty as the reader's deficit mislabels the most interesting cases.

---

## 5 · What lands where

**The response record takes the exchange** — question, its `[in-text]`/`[open]` mark, the user's answer verbatim, their reasoning, your assessment marked as yours, the verdict. Append-only; errors kept at the same prominence as everything else. Format in `store-template.md`.

**The node takes what the questioning established about the unit** — distilled, at the node it bears on. A judgment the user made about the source goes there too, marked `[ME]` and verbatim.

**Across sessions the record becomes a dated account, in the user's own words, of where their understanding of a subject actually stood** — which supports returning to the same point later, asked differently, and seeing what has moved. Nothing else in the system accumulates this. **A notebook, not a gradebook:** no scores, no percentages, no tallies.
